This chapter formalizes the semantics of the two core languages of \GRust: the service-modeling
kernel \GRFRP and the component-modeling kernel \GRSync. For each language, we provide a formal
syntax, an \emph{operational semantics}, a \emph{denotational semantics}, and key theorems that
establish their soundness.

The \emph{operational semantics} models the \emph{execution} of programs. In this chapter, we define
a small-step operational semantics for both \GRFRP and \GRSync. This approach focuses on how
\emph{individual values} are computed as the program executes, making it particularly suited for
modeling dataflow languages, where computations are performed continuously on a flow of inputs.
%
In \GRSync, components are represented as state machines that compute outputs based on inputs and a
state capturing the information needed for future computations (including \last{x} memories and the
state of called components).
%
In \GRFRP, in order to minimize the number of exported messages, services compute by propagating
signal updates and event occurrences to maintain flows up-to-date. The operational
semantics captures this process.
%
% Operational semantics can also be defined in other ways. A \emph{big-step} semantics directly
% describes the computation of the final result of the program, abstracting away intermediate steps.
% In the context of dataflow languages, a big-step semantics can describe how an \emph{entire flow}
% is processed \cite{DBLP:journals/pacmpl/BourkeBP20}. Additionally, the smallest units of computation
% can be captured by a \emph{micro-step} semantics, which models the elementary operations performed
% within a single computation step~\cite{DBLP:books/daglib/0023221}.

The \emph{denotational semantics} provides a mathematical representation of the elements of the
language. It offers an abstract, high-level view of program behavior, focusing on the \emph{what}
(the result) rather than the \emph{how} (the execution). This complements the operational semantics,
which models the step-by-step execution of programs.
%
In dataflow languages like \GRust, each expression is associated with an \emph{infinite} sequence
of values it carries during execution, referred to as a
\emph{stream}~\cite{DBLP:conf/ifip/Kahn74,DBLP:journals/tecs/BourkePP23,DBLP:journals/tcad/LeeS98}.
These streams represent the entire simulation of the expression over time. To maintain a consistent
notion of time while employing the propagation strategy captured by the operational semantics, the
values in the streams of \GRFRP flows are \emph{timestamped}. \Cref{sec:semantics:streams} will give
further explanations on this mathematical model and how it is used in the semantics of \GRSync and
\GRFRP.

The chapter is structured as follows.
\begin{description}
      \item[\Cref{sec:semantics:example}]
            presents an Automatic Emergency Braking (\aeb) system as a running example to provide an
            intuitive understanding of the \GRust semantics formalized in this chapter.

      \item[\Cref{sec:semantics:streams}]
            explains the mathematical model of \emph{streams}---infinite, timestamped dataflows that
            capture the history of values over an entire execution. This model forms the basis for
            the denotational semantics of both \GRSync and \GRFRP.

      \item[\Cref{sec:semantics:grsync}]
            presents the formal semantics of the \GRSync kernel. This section defines its abstract
            syntax, an operational semantics based on state machines, and a denotational semantics
            based on streams. It concludes by establishing the soundness theorems for \GRSync.

      \item[\Cref{sec:semantics:grfrp}]
            presents the formal semantics of the \GRFRP kernel. Following a parallel structure to
            the formalization of \GRSync, it defines the abstract syntax, an operational semantics
            that captures asynchronous dataflow propagation, and a denotational semantics based on
            timestamped streams, concluding with the corresponding soundness theorems.
\end{description}

For both kernels, we establish the following soundness theorems.
\begin{description}
      \item[Productivity] Well-typed services and well-typed, causal components are guaranteed to
            produce a valid output for any given input. This property, referred to as
            \emph{productivity}, is formally stated for the operational semantics in
            \Cref{thm:semantics:grsync:theorems:productivity,%
                  thm:semantics:grfrp:theorems:productivity}.

      \item[Determinism] The output of a construct is uniquely determined by its input history. This
            property, referred to as \emph{determinism}, is formally stated for the denotational
            semantics in
            \Cref{thm:semantics:grsync:theorems:determinism,%
                  thm:semantics:grfrp:theorems:determinism}.

      \item[Equivalence] The operational and denotational semantics are proven to be consistent. The
            execution of a construct, as defined by the operational semantics, yields a result that
            matches its denotational meaning. This \emph{equivalence} is formally stated in
            \Cref{thm:semantics:grsync:theorems:equivalence,%
                  thm:semantics:grfrp:theorems:equivalence}.

      \item[Oversampling] The operational semantics models a propagation-based execution. We prove
            that additional computations, a process referred to as \emph{oversampling}, do not
            alter the output values of flows. This property, stated in
            \Cref{thm:semantics:grsync:theorems:oversampling,%
                  thm:semantics:grfrp:theorems:oversampling}, justifies the safe transition from a
            periodic to an event-driven execution model.
\end{description}
